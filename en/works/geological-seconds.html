Geological Seconds is an installation about the disproportion between two regimes of time: the human, measured in fractions of a second, and the geological, measured in millennia. Photographs are transferred, in cyanotype, directly onto stones: the instant in which a shot is taken ends up imprinted on matter that existed long before the frame.

In a single space the work stages two registers of handling the image. Hundreds of fleeting, all-but-disposable shots — the kind taken by the dozen each day and almost never revisited — are printed on stones and heaped like scree: a stream of images that has acquired weight and cannot be deleted. Rare archival photographs sit on individual stones on pedestals, with museum labels, like valuable exhibits. The same gesture — transfer onto stone — confers opposite statuses: the disposable becomes heavy and permanent, the singular becomes museal. The work unsettles the habitual sense of which moment is worth what.

In a site-specific realization, this question is bound to a particular place: the archival images are drawn from the history of the venue itself, and the stones are gathered there. The human history of a place turns out to be a thin, recent layer on rock that is millennia older.

Cyanotype is light-sensitive and fades over time — the image on the stone is more perishable than the stone itself. Yet the work does not turn this into an answer: the act of transfer and the pedestal are themselves an effort to preserve, an admission that the moment was worth something. Which will prevail — the perishability of the image or the stubbornness of holding onto it, the human gaze or indifferent matter — Geological Seconds does not decide, but holds open as a question, leaving the viewer inside the disproportion.
